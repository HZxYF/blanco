The history, in six eras

Real Madrid's story is usually told as a trophy count. Told as six periods, it makes more sense: each era answers a different question about how a football club survives, and then dominates.

1902–1945 — A club before a league

Madrid Football Club was formally set up on 6 March 1902, when a board was elected and Juan Padrós became president. It was not a rich man's project: the club grew out of students and workers who had been playing on the fields north of the city since the late 1890s.

There was no league to win. The national competition was the cup, and Madrid won it for the first time in 1905 — then again in 1906, 1907 and 1908, four in a row. They won it again in 1917 and 1934, and once more in 1936, months before the Civil War stopped football for three seasons.

In June 1920 Alfonso XIII gave the club the title Real — royal — and a crown went onto the badge. The league finally arrived in 1929: Madrid finished second that first season, then won the title in 1931–32 and retained it a year later. When the war ended the club was rebuilding, and in 1943 Santiago Bernabéu, a former player, became president.

1953–1966 — The team that invented the competition

Bernabéu's plan was simple to state and expensive to carry out: sign the best player available and build a stadium worth watching him in. Alfredo Di Stéfano arrived in 1953, and the Chamartín stadium — renamed for the president in 1955 — became the stage.

The European Cup began in 1955, and Madrid won the first five. The 1960 final, 7–3 against Eintracht Frankfurt at Hampden Park, is still the highest-scoring final ever played; Ferenc Puskás, signed at 31 two years earlier, scored four of the seven.

The sixth came in 1966 with a different team — the Yé-yé side, built on young Spanish players around Francisco Gento, the only survivor of the first five. After that the competition moved on without them for three decades.

1966–1990 — Thirty-two years without the cup

At home the club kept winning. League titles came regularly through the 1970s, and the UEFA Cup was won in 1985 and 1986. From 1986 the side was built around the Quinta del Buitre — five players from the academy, Emilio Butragueño the best known of them — and they won five consecutive league titles.

In Europe it kept going wrong. Madrid reached the 1981 final and lost to Liverpool, and the semi-finals became a habit that ended badly. The club that had won the first five European Cups spent the 1970s and 1980s watching other teams lift it.

1998–2003 — The seventh, and the Galácticos

The seventh European Cup came in 1998, thirty-two years after the sixth: 1–0 against Juventus in Amsterdam. Two more followed quickly — 3–0 against Valencia in 2000, and 2–1 against Bayer Leverkusen in 2002, decided by Zidane's volley.

Off the pitch the club pursued a policy that became a brand: roughly one superstar a year — Figo in 2000, Zidane in 2001, Ronaldo in 2002, Beckham in 2003. It sold shirts and filled the stadium. It did not produce another European Cup for twelve years.

2014–2018 — The Décima, then three in a row

The tenth had become an obsession by the time it arrived in 2014: 4–1 after extra time against Atlético Madrid in Lisbon. Zidane, appointed in January 2016, then won the competition in each of his first three seasons — 2016 on penalties against Atlético, 2017 4–1 against Juventus, 2018 3–1 against Liverpool.

No club had retained the trophy in the Champions League era. Madrid won it three times running, with Cristiano Ronaldo scoring in all three finals.

2022–2024 — A rebuild that finished first

Ronaldo left in 2018 and the team aged. The rebuild was quiet and mostly young: Vinícius Júnior, Rodrygo, Federico Valverde, and later Jude Bellingham. It produced a fourteenth European Cup in 2022 — a run of late comebacks ending 1–0 against Liverpool in Paris — and a fifteenth in 2024, 2–0 against Borussia Dortmund at Wembley.

Fifteen finals, fifteen wins. Whatever else changes about the club, that is the record every other club is measured against.
